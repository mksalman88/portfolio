Placemaking on Chinatown's Forsyth Street

The Benefits of Permeability and the Inevitability of Urban Change

Sara D. Roosevelt Park runs seven blocks along Forsyth Street, from Houston down to Canal. Near Grand Street, where the B and D trains surface from underground, the park is at its most alive. The space is open, permeable, and used — not in the curated sense of a designed plaza, but in the way that happens when a community simply claims a place as its own. Elderly men and women gather around tables and benches, engaging in board- and card-games. Children and teenagers play football and tag. Ping pong tables sit right at the park's edge, open to the pedestrian-only path between Hester and Canal Street. The activity is self-organized and unhurried, placemaking not as intervention but as habit.

The park's character shifts as you move south. By the time Forsyth approaches Canal Street, the neighborhood's geometry and geographical context takes over. Canal, the Bowery, and Christie Street all converge at the foot of the Manhattan Bridge on-ramp — a tangle of traffic, noise, and infrastructure that has no interest in the pedestrian. The bridge doesn't erase Forsyth Street so much as subordinate it: the street continues, but displaced onto a decline, pushed to the margins of the arch and colonnade that mark the bridge's Manhattan entrance. A bollard-lined wedge of pavement, the arch looming overhead, traffic pressing in from every side - Forsyth Street yields to something that was never built with it in mind. To reach the market on the other side, you have to cross through this landscape.

Forsyth Street continues. But the terms have changed. South of the crossing, the street narrows into the shadow of the on-ramp — the bridge no longer a backdrop but an immediate physical presence, pressing down on the block below. The space it produces is not quite street, not quite shelter. Residual, in the truest sense.

Chua Mia Tee's Benjamin Sheares Bridge – The Viaduct (1981) comes to mind, both for its subject and for its mood. The painting renders Singapore's rapid modernization as something vast, indifferent, and alienating: concrete colonnade, glass towers, scraped earth, a patch of green hemmed in on all sides - the human figure is microscopic. Urban scale and change bring with them a sense of alienation and displacement. While Forsyth Market's bustling crowds are more of a subject than the people in Chua Mia Tee's composition, the tension remains unresolved: the bridge is still indifferent; the market exists in spite of it.

Chua Mia Tee, Benjamin Sheares Bridge – The Viaduct, 1981. Collection of the National Gallery Singapore.
